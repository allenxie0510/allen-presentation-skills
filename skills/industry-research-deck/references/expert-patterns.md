# Industry research patterns

## Job-to-be-Done

Help a decision-maker understand how a market is defined, how value and profit
move through it, how large and attractive it may be, how competition works, what
could change, and which strategic choice follows. A report is successful when
claims are traceable and implications survive scrutiny.

## Stable method

1. **Decision and scope:** define user, use, geography, period, product/service
   boundary, customer, and exclusions.
2. **Hypotheses:** state initial beliefs and what evidence would confirm or
   disconfirm them.
3. **Source plan:** combine official statistics and filings, company primary
   material, expert or stakeholder interviews, and reputable secondary research.
4. **Sizing:** use top-down and bottom-up estimates where possible; reconcile
   definitions and show a range rather than false precision.
5. **Structure:** map value chain, pools of value, customer segments, suppliers,
   rivals, entrants, substitutes, regulation, and complements.
6. **Dynamics:** distinguish enduring structure from cyclical change, emerging
   drivers, weak signals, and scenario assumptions.
7. **Implications:** connect evidence to strategic options, risks, indicators,
   and the decision required.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer official data, regulatory filings, audited reports, standards bodies, and
first-party operational disclosures. Then use method-transparent research and
expert interviews. Treat vendor forecasts, press articles, search snippets, and
anonymous estimates as leads requiring verification. Record source date,
definition, geography, units, currency, sample, and method.

Do not average estimates with incompatible market definitions. Build a bridge
that explains inclusion, exclusion, conversion, and timing differences.

## Analytical lenses

- Market definition and segmentation.
- Size, growth, adoption, penetration, and value pools.
- Value chain and bargaining relationships.
- Rivalry, entrants, substitutes, supplier power, and customer power.
- Unit economics, profit pools, switching costs, regulation, and technology.
- Scenarios, leading indicators, and strategic options.

Five Forces is a structure lens, not a five-box decoration. Competition also
requires multiple measures; concentration alone cannot establish intensity.

## Primary expert sources

- OECD, **Methodologies for Market Studies** — information collection,
  analytical methods, and method selection:
  https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/methodologies-for-market-studies_8731d5ef-en.html
- OECD, **Market Studies Guide for Competition Authorities** — scoping,
  hypotheses, preliminary evidence, stakeholder input, iterative methods, and
  analysis:
  https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/03/market-studies-guide-for-competition-authorities_d7675da2/7381b582-en.pdf
- UK Competition and Markets Authority, **Approach to market studies and
  investigations** — formal market-study and evidence process:
  https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/market-studies-and-market-investigations-supplemental-guidance-on-the-cmas-approach
- Michael Porter, **The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy** — industry
  structure extends beyond direct rivals to buyers, suppliers, entrants, and
  substitutes:
  https://hbr.org/2008/01/the-five-competitive-forces-that-shape-strategy
- OECD, **Methodologies to Measure Market Competition** — use a suite of
  indicators and interpret limitations:
  https://www.oecd.org/en/events/2021/06/methodologies-to-measure-market-competition.html
- U.S. SEC, **EDGAR APIs** — primary filings and XBRL company facts, with period
  alignment caveats:
  https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces
